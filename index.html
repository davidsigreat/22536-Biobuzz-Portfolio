Our school & mission

We are five students from Saint Louis School in Honolulu, Hawaiʻi, with an interest in engineering and making things work. Our school motto, “Mindful and Faithful” (Memor et Fidelis), reminds us to be thoughtful about our work and dependable when teammates or other students need help.

A family connection

David S. and Daniel S. are brothers; Dylan B. and Devyn B. are brothers. David and Dylan are the older siblings. Jayden is the fifth member of the team. The brother connections make our group distinctive, but what matters most is how all five of us contribute.

How we work together

With a small team, nobody stays in only one role. Building, CAD, programming, outreach, and documentation overlap throughout the week. We share tasks, ask questions, and make decisions together. That is especially important when a mechanical change affects code or when a problem shows up during practice.

How we stayed organized

Our weekly schedule covered building, programming, testing, outreach, and competition preparation. We set small goals, divided tasks based on what each member knew, and discussed how testing changed our priorities. After each competition, we planned a quick recap to talk about what went well, what needed work, and how to improve before the next event. Because everyone worked in more than one area, we had to communicate often and avoid letting one unfinished subsystem stop the rest of the robot.

Robot Overview / System Architecture

BioBuzz brings the intake, drivetrain, electronics, and rotating turret into a compact frame. We arranged the mechanisms so the robot could collect game elements, move them through the robot, and aim from different angles without constantly turning its entire chassis.

Energy chain
Rotating turret
Pocketed aluminum
Front intake
Design priorities
Compact & practical

Our design decisions were shaped by how the systems fit together. The pocketed side plates support the chassis while providing access to the mechanical components. The top assembly holds the turret and cable management. Custom-printed parts give us the ability to create and modify specific shapes without redesigning every part of the robot.

How it flows

The front rollers collect the game pieces. An internal path leads toward the scoring mechanism. The turret helps position that mechanism in different directions. These systems must work together, so we evaluate the complete path—not just one part at a time.

Design inspiration

Our design started with the game's collection and scoring requirements. We developed a roller intake to collect pollen and tested grip tape and adjustments to improve its grip. The rotating turret came from our goal of aiming from different angles without turning the entire chassis.

Intake / An Engineering Case Study

The problem

We wanted an intake that picks up pollen anywhere across the front without the driver lining up perfectly. In testing, the intake kept jamming when two pollen reached the converge point, where the intake narrows to index pieces into the robot, at the same time. We needed the pieces to feed in one after the other instead of getting stuck.

Custom Parts / Manufacturing

Structural choices

Our aluminum side plates have pocketed cutouts instead of remaining solid. The open pattern gives access to mechanisms and reduces material while maintaining the overall frame shape. We designed around a tight package: drivetrain, intake, turret, and electronics all need space without getting in each other’s way.

What we made

Drivetrain pulleys

Guides cables through turret movement.

Why we mix processes

Our team uses manufactured metal pieces alongside printed parts. Each approach solves a different problem. Printing helps with custom shapes and revisions; machined metal provides the structure we need. We still have to check tolerances, mounting, and service access once everything is assembled.

Electrical / Software / Reliability

Wiring diagram
Wiring + routine checks

Both hubs sit beneath the top plate. We route wires away from moving parts, especially around the turret, and keep connectors accessible. Before each practice or match we check power, cable routing, belts, fasteners, and clearances.

Software makes the hardware useful

Our programming work covers driver control, motor behavior, and autonomous movement. We develop a routine, test it on the physical robot, watch what actually happens, and revise the code when the result does not match the plan. Changes to mechanical hardware often require software adjustments, so programmers and builders test together.

Testing / Learning From Each Run

Why we test the complete robot

A mechanism working on the bench does not automatically mean it will work in a match. We practiced driving, tried the intake with actual game elements, and looked at how the systems behaved together. When something went wrong, we talked through whether the cause was mechanical, electrical, or software-related.

Pedro Pathing / Autonomous

Our autonomous approach

We use Pedro Pathing to develop our autonomous movement, along with odometry pods for position tracking. Instead of relying only on timed motor commands, we can plan how the robot should move and use localization feedback while testing the path.

How we build an autonomous routine
Plan path
Track position
Run robot
Tune
Odometry / learning where we are

The odometry pods give our program information related to the robot’s movement. That makes them useful when we are testing whether a path follows the intended route. The system still depends on correct setup, tuning, and repeatable tests; adding odometry does not automatically make autonomous accurate.

How we verify the code

We want to compare the planned route with what the robot actually does on the field. Our notes should include where the robot started, which path we ran, whether it finished, and what we changed afterward. Repeating the same test helps separate a real fix from one lucky run.

Why a turret?

The turret gives our scoring mechanism a way to turn without rotating the entire drivetrain every time. We described the range as roughly 180 degrees to the left and 180 to the right, but a small dead zone means the usable range is about 355 degrees of usable rotation.

Motor, gearing and cable routing
How we built the assembly

Our turret uses Axon MAX servos and 3D-printed gears for rotation. We also made a printed hood and energy chain. The chain guides the wires while the assembly moves, helping us keep cables away from the rotating parts. These are custom design choices that require careful clearance and maintenance checks.

Drivetrain / Power / Serviceability

Drive decisions

Our robot uses goBILDA motors for the drive. We also use custom 3D-printed pulleys. The pocketed aluminum plates help organize the drivetrain and support the mechanisms above it.

From a compact layout to reliable matches
Mechanical maintenance

A compact chassis makes the robot easier to maneuver, but leaves less room for hands and tools. We check that moving parts clear nearby wiring, the belts remain aligned, and fasteners can be reached. A design that works only when everything is brand new is not practical for repeated matches.

Farrington / Technical Mentorship

Programming help
Explaining the problem, not just the answer

We worked with Farrington students on programming. We tried to understand what their robot or code was supposed to do, discuss possible causes of the problem, and explain different ways to troubleshoot. Mentoring another FTC team is different from fixing our own robot because we need to communicate the reasoning clearly.

Electrical support

We also helped with electrical work. We talked through connections, how components interact, and what could be checked when hardware does not behave as expected. We want the students we mentor to understand the steps so they can investigate future issues themselves, rather than depending on us every time something goes wrong.

What we learned from helping another team

The collaboration pushed us to practice patience, technical communication, and teamwork with students outside Saint Louis School. We also saw how explaining a concept can reveal gaps in our own understanding.

AI-Assisted Programming / Our Plan

The challenge we noticed

Students who are new to FTC programming may know what they want their robot to do but struggle to turn that idea into Java. It can be discouraging when an error message appears and nobody knows where to begin. We want AI to be a learning resource that helps students ask clearer questions, understand code, and explore debugging steps.

A responsible AI learning cycle
Explain
Ask
Verify
Test
What we want to offer

Our idea includes short workshops, mentoring, and a beginner-friendly programming guide. It could show example prompts for understanding unfamiliar Java code, explaining errors, and thinking through robot behavior. Students would still have to check suggestions against their code, hardware, and the FTC rules before using them.

What success would mean

A successful workshop would leave students more confident in their ability to understand and debug their own program. We plan to ask participants about their confidence before and afterward, observe whether they can explain a solution, and learn which examples were helpful.

Our status

The Farrington programming and electrical help is completed outreach. The wider AI workshop program and guide remain an initiative in development.

Growing as a Five-Member Team

Learning by doing

We have learned to connect CAD and physical manufacturing, to test code on a real robot, and to recognize that a problem rarely belongs to only one subsystem. The intake problem, for example, required us to think about geometry, friction, and how the drivers approached the game elements—not just add another material.

What each part of the team contributes
CAD / design

Planning the shape and location of mechanisms before making parts.

Build / manufacture

Assembling the robot, checking clearances, and changing hardware.

Programming / electrical

Connecting code to actual movement and managing the control system.

Outreach / documentation

Organizing tasks, explaining work, and recording what we learn.

Making future seasons easier

We want to document the robot, preserve our code and CAD, organize our tools and parts, and share lessons with whoever joins next. We also try to reuse materials where practical. Sustainability for our team means having people who can explain the work, not just a machine that survives one tournament.
